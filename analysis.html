Reading CLP Holdings' FY2023–FY2025 results

Three years, four numbers each, one honest story: a headline "profit surges 76%" in 2024 that mostly wasn't a turnaround at all. Here's what I actually took away from CLP's numbers, and what I'd still want to check if I were covering this name properly.

Executive summary

CLP's FY2023 statutory profit ("Total Earnings") was HK$6.7bn. FY2024's was HK$11.7bn, a 76% jump that made headlines. Read at face value, that looks like a business that turned itself around in a year. It didn't, not really. FY2023's low number was mostly one thing: a HK$5.9bn non-cash writedown on the value of EnergyAustralia's customer relationships, disclosed in a profit warning a few weeks before the full FY2023 results. Strip that one-off out, using CLP's own "Operating Earnings" measure instead, and the picture flips: the underlying business was actually slightly weaker in 2024 than 2023, and weaker again in 2025. Two profit lines, same company, same year, telling close to opposite stories, and the more dramatic one is the one that made the news.

1. Why FY2023 looked so bad on the statutory line

In January 2024, ahead of its full-year results, CLP issued a profit warning: it expected to record a HK$5.9bn impairment of goodwill attached to EnergyAustralia's Customer business. The company's own explanation was that tougher economic and operating conditions had forced a revision of its assumptions on retail margins, customer growth, and EnergyAustralia's cost of capital, with retail margins doing most of the damage. That's an accounting write-down, not a cash outflow. Nothing was sold, nothing changed hands. It's CLP formally admitting that a business it paid for years ago is now worth less on paper than it once assumed. But because "Total Earnings" is the statutory, after-everything number, that HK$5.9bn hit landed directly on FY2023's bottom line, dragging it down to HK$6.7bn even though CLP's own "Operating Earnings" measure, which excludes exactly this kind of one-off item, came in at HK$12.3bn that same year, roughly double.

What I kept coming back to: a HK$5.9bn "loss" that's really a revaluation, not a cheque anyone wrote, can make a genuinely fine year look like a crisis on the one line most headlines quote.

2. The "76% profit surge" in 2024 is mostly a base effect

Once you know FY2023 was artificially depressed by a one-off writedown, FY2024's 76% jump in Total Earnings reads differently. Most of that jump is just FY2023's impairment not repeating, not FY2024 being a dramatically better year of actually running the business. The more telling number is Operating Earnings, CLP's own measure of the core business excluding one-off items like the impairment: it went from HK$12.3bn in 2023 to HK$11.6bn in 2024, a decline of about 5%, and fell again to HK$10.9bn in 2025, another 6% down. So while the statutory headline was "profit surges 76%," the steadier, arguably more honest measure was quietly declining both years. I don't think that makes CLP a bad business, utilities aren't supposed to be exciting, but it's a good example of why the number that makes the headline and the number that describes the business aren't always the same one.

3. Revenue moved for a completely different reason

It would be neat if revenue and profit told a matching story here, but they mostly don't, and not because of anything as dramatic as the impairment. Revenue rose 4.4% in 2024, to HK$91.0bn, on higher wholesale power prices in Australia, then eased back 3.2% in 2025, to HK$88.0bn, as those prices came off their highs. That's a straightforward commodity-price effect on the top line, unrelated to the accounting story playing out further down the income statement. The lesson I'd draw generally: revenue, statutory profit, and underlying operating profit can each be responding to a different driver in the same year, and reading only one of them will usually get you a version of the story that's true but incomplete.

4. The dividend didn't wait for the headline number to recover

CLP raised its dividend per share in both years shown here: HK$3.10 in 2023, HK$3.15 in 2024, HK$3.20 in 2025. It raised the payout in 2024 even though the "recovery" in Total Earnings that year was substantially a base effect rather than a real improvement in the underlying business, which suggests the board was looking at Operating Earnings and the cash the business was actually throwing off, not the statutory headline everyone else was reading. A steady, modest dividend increase through a year when the core measure was quietly declining is a specific kind of signal: it says management didn't think the FY2023 writedown, or the FY2024 "recovery," changed much about the business's ability to keep paying out.

5. What I'd still want to check

A few things I couldn't fully resolve from public disclosures alone:

  • Whether the assumptions behind the FY2023 impairment (retail margins, customer growth, cost of capital for EnergyAustralia) have stabilized, or whether a further writedown is plausible if the Australian retail market keeps getting tougher.
  • Why Operating Earnings fell in both 2024 and 2025 even as revenue rose in 2024, what's compressing margins on the core business specifically, separate from the one-off items both profit measures are designed to exclude.
  • How much of the FY2025 decline is Australia-specific versus a broader softening across CLP's Hong Kong, Mainland, and other Asian operations, since the three-year, four-metric view here doesn't break the group figure down by segment.

A note on the data, and why this page is narrower than it used to be

This page originally covered six years (FY2020-FY2025) and several more ratios, including ROE, gearing, and interest coverage. Two rounds of fact-checking, the first from a reader's spot-check against CLP's own 10-year summary, the second a full line-by-line re-verification I did in response, turned up a series of genuine errors along the way, including one where I'd conflated two similarly-named CLP metrics, "Operating Earnings" and "Operating Earnings before fair value movements", which are both real numbers CLP reports, just not the same number. Every fix along the way was documented rather than quietly patched. But the deeper lesson was that six years and eight metrics, pulled from search snippets rather than primary documents I could read end to end, created more opportunities for exactly that kind of mix-up. So the scope here was deliberately cut to three years and four metrics, each checked directly against CLP's own results announcements and presentations (with page references, not just search results), rather than a wider page with more residual uncertainty. Reliability here comes from a narrower, consistent basis, not from covering more ground. This is a personal project, not something CLP has reviewed or approved. See the disclaimer below.
