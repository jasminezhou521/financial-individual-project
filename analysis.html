Reading CLP Holdings' FY2020–FY2025 results

Six years, one energy-market shock, and a dividend that never wavered. Here's what I actually took away from CLP's numbers, and what I'd still want to check if I were covering this name properly.

Executive summary

When I first pulled CLP's numbers, my honest reaction was that this looked like a company having a rough few years. Revenue swings from HK$79.6bn up to HK$100.7bn and back down to HK$88.0bn. Statutory profit, which CLP calls "Total Earnings," fell 92% in a single year. Read at face value, that's a business in trouble. Once I dug into what actually happened in 2022 though, the picture changed a lot. It's really one story: a hedging and plant-outage problem in CLP's Australian retail arm, EnergyAustralia, that hit hard in 2022 and mostly unwound over the following two years. Underneath that, the core Hong Kong, Mainland and Asia infrastructure business kept generating cash the whole time, the dividend never got cut, and the balance sheet stayed conservative. That's really what this exercise is about for me: not just finding the numbers, but working out which ones are actually telling you something and which ones are noise.

1. Revenue and profit went in opposite directions in 2022

Revenue actually peaked in 2022, hitting HK$100.7bn, up 20% on the year before. If you stopped reading there, you'd think 2022 was CLP's best year on record. It really wasn't. Total Earnings fell from HK$8.6bn to just HK$0.9bn in the same period, a 92% drop. Here's what happened. EnergyAustralia had sold forward contracts at prices agreed before Australia's 2022 energy crisis hit. Then unplanned outages knocked out generation at the Yallourn plant in Victoria and Mount Piper in New South Wales, so CLP had to buy replacement power on the spot market at prices way above what it had already agreed to sell at. That mismatch produced a HK$2.9bn fair-value loss on energy derivatives, which hit profit hard. Revenue moved the other way for a more general reason: 2022 was a year of unusually high energy prices across the board, and CLP's other, non-hedged businesses sold into that high-price environment, which lifted the group's top line even as EnergyAustralia's fixed-price contracts turned into a loss. Same root cause, two different channels. So now when I see "revenue growth," my first question is where it came from. Revenue that grows because prices are simply higher everywhere doesn't tell you whether the business underneath it is actually doing better.

What I kept coming back to: for a business exposed to commodity prices, revenue going up isn't automatically good news. You have to ask why it grew before you can say whether it's actually good news or bad news wearing a good-news number.

2. Why CLP reports two different profit numbers

Every year, CLP publishes both Total Earnings (the statutory number under IFRS/HKFRS) and Operating Earnings, its own adjusted measure that strips out fair-value swings on energy derivatives and other one-off items. 2022 was rough on both: Total Earnings fell 92% to HK$0.9bn, and Operating Earnings fell 51% too, to HK$4.6bn from HK$9.5bn the year before. But the gap between the two measures is still the interesting part: even in the worst year, the underlying business was still generating roughly five times what the statutory number showed, because a big chunk of the statutory loss was a mark-to-market hit on contracts CLP still expected to net out over their life, not money that was actually gone for good. By 2024 the two measures had converged again as the hedge book worked its way back to normal, and by then Operating Earnings had more than doubled from its 2022 low. I think the useful habit here is a general one, not specific to CLP: whenever a company shows you an adjusted number, ask what it's leaving out and whether that's a fair thing to leave out or just a convenient one. In CLP's case I'd call the 2022 adjustment fair. It's a specific, disclosed, one-off combination of plant outages and a commodity price spike, not a recurring cost being quietly waved away, and it didn't spare Operating Earnings either, it just hit it less hard.

3. The dividend might be the more honest signal

CLP held its dividend flat at HK$3.10 per share for four years straight, 2020 through 2023, which spans the entire 2022 shock, then raised it to HK$3.15 in 2024 and HK$3.20 in 2025 once earnings had actually recovered. I'll admit my first instinct was to look for a "dividend kept rising every year" story, because that's the tidy narrative. The real pattern is less dramatic but arguably more disciplined: management didn't cut the payout when profit collapsed 92%, and it didn't raise it either until the recovery was real and visible in the numbers, not just hoped for. That's a more conservative signal than a mechanical annual increase would be. It says the board treated 2022 as a shock to absorb without panicking, but also didn't want to lock in a higher payout on the back of one bad year's uncertainty about how fast things would bounce back. If I were building an investment case for an infrastructure or utility name, I'd read a flat-then-resumed dividend like this as a sign of a board being deliberate, not just optimistic.

4. The balance sheet backs that confidence up

CLP's latest disclosed numbers, from FY2024, show debt to equity of roughly 0.58x and funds-from-operations interest cover of 11 times. Neither number is dramatic, and honestly that's the point. This is a business financed conservatively enough to absorb something like the 2022 shock without touching its dividend or its credit standing. Moderate leverage, strong interest coverage, and mostly contracted or regulated revenue in the core Hong Kong business is roughly the profile infrastructure lenders and investors look for, and it's a big part of why CLP could treat 2022 as a one-off loss to absorb rather than a crisis to manage.

5. What I'd still want to check

A few things I couldn't fully resolve from public disclosures alone, and would want to ask about directly:

  • Has EnergyAustralia restructured its hedging book since 2022 to reduce the odds of the same mismatch happening again, or was that genuinely a one-off combination of bad luck?
  • FY2025's earnings also dipped partly because of lower nuclear and renewables contribution on the Mainland. Is that a one-year timing effect, like maintenance outages or a tariff reset, or something more structural I should be worried about?
  • CLP has talked about significant capex ahead for grid upgrades and renewables. I'd want to watch whether the interest cover and gearing ratio stay this comfortable as that spending ramps up, since a regulated-return framework only protects profitability if the regulatory reset actually keeps pace with the investment.
  • As a capital-intensive business with a lot of project financing, CLP's borrowing costs are exposed to the direction of global rates in a way its Hong Kong regulated-return scheme only partly offsets.

A note on the data and its limits

Every figure here comes from CLP's own Annual Results announcements and investor materials for FY2020 through FY2025, cross-checked against other financial data sources where I couldn't pull CLP's own PDF directly during this exercise. An earlier version of this page had two real errors that I want to own rather than quietly fix: FY2022 Operating Earnings was overstated at HK$7.6bn (the correct figure is HK$4.6bn), and the dividend-per-share series showed a steady rise from 2020 to 2023 that never actually happened, the dividend was flat at HK$3.10 that whole time. Both were caught by a manual fact-check against CLP's own 10-year financial summary and corrected. One number I still flag as not fully certain: the FY2024 ROE figure is a third-party estimate on a slightly different profit and equity base than the ones I calculated myself for 2021 to 2023.
