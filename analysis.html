Reading CLP Holdings' FY2020–FY2025 results

Six years, one energy-market shock, and a dividend that never wavered. Here's what I actually took away from CLP's numbers, and what I'd still want to check if I were covering this name properly.

Executive summary

When I first pulled CLP's numbers, my honest reaction was that this looked like a company having a rough few years. Revenue swings from HK$79.6bn up to HK$100.7bn and back down to HK$88.0bn. Statutory profit, which CLP calls "Total Earnings," fell 92% in a single year. Read at face value, that's a business in trouble. Once I dug into what actually happened in 2022 though, the picture changed a lot. It's really one story: a hedging and plant-outage problem in CLP's Australian retail arm, EnergyAustralia, that hit hard in 2022 and mostly unwound over the following two years. Underneath that, the core Hong Kong, Mainland and Asia infrastructure business kept generating cash the whole time, the dividend never got cut, and the balance sheet stayed conservative. That's really what this exercise is about for me: not just finding the numbers, but working out which ones are actually telling you something and which ones are noise.

1. Revenue and profit went in opposite directions in 2022

Revenue actually peaked in 2022, hitting HK$100.7bn, up 20% on the year before. If you stopped reading there, you'd think 2022 was CLP's best year on record. It really wasn't. Total Earnings fell from HK$8.6bn to just HK$0.9bn in the same period, a 92% drop. Here's what happened. EnergyAustralia had sold forward contracts at prices agreed before Australia's 2022 energy crisis hit. Then unplanned outages knocked out generation at the Yallourn plant in Victoria and Mount Piper in New South Wales, so CLP had to buy replacement power on the spot market at prices way above what it had already agreed to sell at. That mismatch alone produced a HK$2.9bn fair-value loss on energy derivatives, and because of how that flows through the accounts, it pushed revenue up and profit down at the same time. So now when I see "revenue growth," my first question is where it came from. Revenue that grows because a company is being forced to pay more for something it needs isn't really growth at all, even though it shows up looking exactly like growth on the top line.

What I kept coming back to: for a business exposed to commodity prices, revenue going up isn't automatically good news. You have to ask why it grew before you can say whether it's actually good news or bad news wearing a good-news number.

2. Why CLP reports two different profit numbers

Every year, CLP publishes both Total Earnings (the statutory number under IFRS/HKFRS) and Operating Earnings, its own adjusted measure that strips out fair-value swings on energy derivatives and other one-off items. In 2022 those two numbers told almost opposite stories: HK$0.9bn statutory against HK$7.6bn underlying. That gap exists because the derivative loss was mostly a timing issue, a mark-to-market effect on contracts CLP still expected to net out over their life, rather than money that was actually gone for good. By 2023 and 2024 the two measures had mostly converged again as the hedge book worked its way back to normal. I think the useful habit here is a general one, not specific to CLP: whenever a company shows you an adjusted number, ask what it's leaving out and whether that's a fair thing to leave out or just a convenient one. In CLP's case I'd call the 2022 adjustment fair. It's a specific, disclosed, one-off combination of plant outages and a commodity price spike, not a recurring cost being quietly waved away.

3. The dividend might be the more honest signal

CLP raised its dividend per share every single year from 2020 through 2025, including 2022, the year statutory profit fell more than 90%. I don't think that's a coincidence or just a stubborn habit. A board doesn't keep raising a payout through a 92% earnings collapse unless it genuinely believes the collapse is temporary and the underlying business is fine. If I were building an investment case for an infrastructure or utility name, I'd honestly weight the dividend track record more heavily than any single year's statutory profit line. It's management putting its money where its mouth is, and it turned out to be the right call.

4. The balance sheet backs that confidence up

CLP's latest disclosed numbers, from FY2024, show debt to equity of roughly 0.58x and funds-from-operations interest cover of 11 times. Neither number is dramatic, and honestly that's the point. This is a business financed conservatively enough to absorb something like the 2022 shock without touching its dividend or its credit standing. Moderate leverage, strong interest coverage, and mostly contracted or regulated revenue in the core Hong Kong business is roughly the profile infrastructure lenders and investors look for, and it's a big part of why CLP could treat 2022 as a one-off loss to absorb rather than a crisis to manage.

5. What I'd still want to check

A few things I couldn't fully resolve from public disclosures alone, and would want to ask about directly:

  • Has EnergyAustralia restructured its hedging book since 2022 to reduce the odds of the same mismatch happening again, or was that genuinely a one-off combination of bad luck?
  • FY2025's earnings also dipped partly because of lower nuclear and renewables contribution on the Mainland. Is that a one-year timing effect, like maintenance outages or a tariff reset, or something more structural I should be worried about?
  • CLP has talked about significant capex ahead for grid upgrades and renewables. I'd want to watch whether the interest cover and gearing ratio stay this comfortable as that spending ramps up, since a regulated-return framework only protects profitability if the regulatory reset actually keeps pace with the investment.
  • As a capital-intensive business with a lot of project financing, CLP's borrowing costs are exposed to the direction of global rates in a way its Hong Kong regulated-return scheme only partly offsets.

A note on the data and its limits

Every figure here comes from CLP's own Annual Results announcements and investor materials for FY2020 through FY2025, cross-checked against other financial data sources where I couldn't pull CLP's own PDF directly during this exercise. Two numbers I want to flag specifically as not fully certain. The FY2024 ROE figure is a third-party estimate on a slightly different profit and equity base than the ones I calculated myself for 2021 to 2023. And the FY2024 dividend per share: two sources put it at HK$3.15, but one investor-presentation source showed HK$3.02 for the same year. I've flagged both rather than just picking one number and presenting it as settled fact. This is a personal project, not something CLP has reviewed or approved. See the disclaimer below.
